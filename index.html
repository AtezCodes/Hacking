01 What is hacking?

At its core, hacking means using technical skill and creativity to understand a system more deeply than its designers intended. It started as a playful term among engineers who loved clever solutions, and it grew into a global discipline that now protects banks, hospitals, governments and everyday people.

Modern security professionals call this work penetration testing, red teaming, vulnerability research or bug bounty hunting. They are paid to think like an attacker so that real attackers find nothing left to exploit.

Curiosity

Every great hacker asks one question constantly: what happens if I do this instead?

Persistence

Most problems fall after hours of failed attempts. Patience beats raw talent.

Ethics

Skill without permission is a crime. Skill with consent is a career.

03 A short history

1960s

MIT and the first hackers

Students at MIT's Tech Model Railroad Club use the word hack for an elegant, clever technical solution.

1971

Phone phreaking

Explorers discover how to manipulate the telephone network with tones, sparking the first wave of hacker culture.

1988

The Morris Worm

One of the first internet worms spreads across thousands of machines and leads to the creation of the first CERT.

1993

DEF CON begins

A small party in Las Vegas grows into the largest hacker conference in the world.

2010s

Bug bounties go mainstream

Companies begin paying researchers openly to find flaws, turning curiosity into a respected profession.

Today

Security everywhere

Cloud, mobile, AI and connected devices make security skills some of the most valuable in technology.

06 Stay protected

Knowing how attacks work is useless if you ignore the basics.

  • Use a password manager and a unique password for every account.
  • Turn on multi-factor authentication, preferably with an app or hardware key.
  • Install updates quickly. Most breaches use flaws that already have a fix.
  • Treat unexpected links and attachments with suspicion, even from people you know.
  • Back up important files and keep one copy offline.
  • Only test systems you own or have written permission to test.
